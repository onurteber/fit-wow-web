6 Common Weight Loss Myths vs. What Science Actually Says

Do carbs really make you fat? Is eating late at night bad? Do you have to count calories? We put the 6 most common weight-loss myths to the test against the actual science.

July 31, 2026 · 6 min read

There is so much conflicting advice about weight loss online that it is hard to know who to trust. In this article we go through the 6 most common myths one by one and explain the science behind each of them.

Myth 1: Carbs Make You Fat

This is one of the most common and most misunderstood myths out there. What determines weight gain is not which food group you eat, but your total daily calorie intake. Excess carbs turn into weight gain only if they push you into a calorie surplus, exactly like excess protein or excess fat would. Low-carb diets work not because carbs are inherently "bad," but usually because they also happen to lower total calorie intake.

Myth 2: Eating After a Certain Time at Night Makes You Gain Weight

We have all heard the "don't eat after 6pm" advice. But your body stores fat based on total energy intake, not the clock. Someone who eats late but stays within their daily calorie target will not gain weight. The real issue is usually the extra snacking, often out of boredom or habit, that gets added on top of the rest of the day's intake.

Myth 3: The Less You Eat, the Faster You Lose Weight

Extremely low-calorie diets can produce fast results in the short term, but they are not sustainable and usually backfire. Very low intake leads to muscle loss, a slower metabolism, constant hunger, and eventually quitting the diet and regaining the weight. A healthy calorie deficit is generally 10 to 20 percent below your TDEE; bigger deficits are risky.

Myth 4: Certain Foods "Boost" Your Metabolism

Claims about green tea, ginger, cinnamon, or apple cider vinegar boosting metabolism come up constantly. Some of these foods may have a measurable but very small effect on metabolism, typically no more than a few calories a day. What actually determines your weight loss is not these "miracle" foods but a consistent calorie deficit.

Myth 5: You Must Do Intense Exercise to Lose Weight

Exercise is valuable for health and helps preserve muscle mass, but it is not a prerequisite for weight loss. Weight loss is primarily determined in the kitchen, through calorie balance. Someone who never exercises but is in a calorie deficit will lose weight; someone who exercises intensely but eats a surplus will not. Exercise is a valuable tool for supporting a calorie deficit and preserving muscle; on its own it is not enough.

Myth 6: Counting Calories Is Unnecessary, Just Listen to Your Body

Intuitive eating can work for some people, but research shows most people underestimate their portion sizes and calorie intake. This is one of the most common reasons behind "why am I not losing weight." A simple tracking method, whether it is writing things down by hand or using an app, makes your actual intake visible and lets you adjust toward your goal.

So What Actually Works?

Once you set the myths aside, only one fact remains: weight loss depends on eating less than you burn. Everything else, meal timing, "forbidden" foods, miracle supplements, either supports that basic balance or makes no difference at all. The hardest part is usually not knowing what to eat, but accurately tracking how much you are eating. Using an app that logs your calories in seconds from a photo of your plate can make that tracking far more sustainable than writing everything down by hand.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do carbs really make you fat?

No. Weight gain is determined by total calorie intake, not by which food group it comes from. Excess carbs, excess protein, and excess fat all cause weight gain when they add up to a calorie surplus. Carbs alone are not the culprit.

Does eating late at night cause weight gain?

It is total daily calorie intake, not meal timing, that determines whether you gain or lose weight. Someone who eats late but stays within their daily calorie target will not gain weight; the real issue is usually the extra snacking that happens late at night.

Do you have to count calories to lose weight?

It is not strictly required, but it is the most reliable method. Intuitive eating works for some people, but most people underestimate their portion sizes and calorie intake. A simple tracking method lets you see your actual intake and adjust toward your goal.
