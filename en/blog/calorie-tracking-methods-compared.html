5 Calorie-Tracking Methods Compared: Which One Is Right for You?

From handwritten logs to AI-powered photo scanning, we compare 5 calorie-tracking methods on accuracy, speed, and how easy they are to stick with.

July 31, 2026 · 6 min read

Why the Tracking Method You Choose Matters

The only way to build a calorie deficit is to know what you're eating, but how you track it directly determines how long you'll keep it up. Pick a method that's too complicated and you'll quit within days; pick one that's too loose and you won't get results. Below we compare the 5 most common methods; in general, the less manual data entry a method requires, the longer people stick with it.

1. Handwritten Logging (Paper or Notes App)

Writing down everything you eat in a notebook or phone note is the oldest and cheapest method. But since most people don't know calorie values by heart, estimates typically run 20-40% off the real number. Because it's time-consuming and inaccurate, it's one of the hardest methods to keep up long term.

2. Barcode-Scanning Apps

Apps that read a packaged product's barcode and pull its nutrition data automatically are quite accurate for packaged foods. The problem is that home-cooked meals, restaurant dishes, and buffets don't have barcodes, so you're back to manual searching and estimating for those.

3. Weighing Food (Kitchen Scale)

Weighing every meal in grams before cooking or plating, then calculating from there, is the most accurate method available. It's common among athletes and bodybuilders. But it's nearly impossible to do while eating out or in social settings, which makes it unsustainable for most people's daily lives.

4. Eyeballing / Portion Estimation

Experienced trackers estimate portions roughly using hand measurements (for example, "a fistful of rice is about 150 grams"). It's fast, but accuracy depends entirely on the user's experience; beginners can be seriously off, and it takes time to calibrate.

5. AI-Powered Photo Scanning

Modern apps that have become popular in recent years only require a photo of your plate; AI recognizes the food and automatically estimates the portion and calories. It works for both packaged and home-cooked meals, requires no manual entry, and takes seconds. It's not quite as precise as a kitchen scale, but for most people it's the best balance of accuracy and practicality, because a method you can't sustain doesn't help you even if it's technically the most accurate. Which One Should You Choose?

If you have a short-term, highly precise goal (say, you're a competitive athlete prepping for a show), a kitchen scale gives the most accurate result. But for most people, the deciding factor isn't accuracy, it's sustainability: a method you actually keep doing beats a theoretically "more accurate" one you abandon after two weeks. That's why AI-powered photo scanning, which fits into daily life with the least friction, is the most realistic winner for most users. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the most accurate calorie-tracking method?

Weighing food on a kitchen scale and calculating by the gram gives the highest accuracy, but it's hard to keep up in everyday life. AI-powered photo scanning offers the best balance of accuracy and practicality for most people.

What's the difference between barcode scanning and photo scanning?

Barcode scanning only works for packaged products and requires manual entry for home-cooked meals or restaurant dishes. AI photo scanning recognizes the food directly on your plate and estimates the portion, so it works for both packaged and home-cooked meals.

Which method should someone new to calorie tracking choose?

For beginners, the most sustainable method is the one that fits into daily life with the least friction. Apps like FitWow that scan a photo of your meal are the easiest to turn into a habit, since they don't require manual data entry.
